<p><strong>Key takeaways</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Training</strong> is the expensive process of teaching a model language patterns from scratch.</li>
<li><strong>Fine-tuning</strong> is adapting an existing model to a specific style or task.</li>
<li><strong>Inference</strong> is using the model to generate text. This is where 99% of business applications live.</li>
<li>A model does not &quot;learn&quot; from your prompts during inference; it only uses them as temporary context.</li>
<li>Most business customization happens via <strong>Context (RAG)</strong>, not training.</li>
</ul>

<p>There is a persistent myth that to use AI for your business, you need to &quot;train&quot; it on your data. This is almost always wrong.</p>
<p>Understanding the difference between training, fine-tuning, and inference is critical for making the right architectural decisions. It is the difference between building a car engine (Training), tuning the suspension for a race (Fine-tuning), and driving to the grocery store (Inference).</p>
<h3 id="what-is-the-difference-between-training-fine-tuning-and-inference">What is the difference between training, fine-tuning, and inference?</h3>
<p>Training creates the base model, fine-tuning adjusts behavior, and inference is the runtime where almost all product value is delivered. This page is for teams trying to avoid expensive architectural confusion, and the key decision is usually whether a problem needs better context and retrieval rather than a new model-training effort.</p>
<p>In practice, <span class="highlight">clarity at boundaries reduces downstream errors</span> more than late-stage tuning.</p>
<aside class="callout callout-insight">
  <p><strong>Design note.</strong> Read this page as a tradeoff map: make boundaries explicit, track feedback loops, and optimize only after constraints are clear.</p>
</aside>

<h2 id="act-i-the-fundamentals">Act I: The fundamentals</h2>
<h3 id="training-building-the-brain">Training: Building the Brain</h3>
<p><strong>Pre-training</strong> is the process of creating a base model (like GPT-4 or Claude). It involves feeding a neural network trillions of tokens of text from the internet.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Goal:</strong> Teach the model the structure of language, facts about the world, and reasoning patterns.</li>
<li><strong>Cost:</strong> Millions of dollars.</li>
<li><strong>Outcome:</strong> A &quot;Base Model&quot; that can predict the next word but has no specific personality or instruction-following ability.</li>
</ul>
<p>You will likely never do this.</p>
<h3 id="fine-tuning-specialized-education">Fine-Tuning: Specialized Education</h3>
<p><strong>Fine-tuning</strong> takes a base model and trains it further on a smaller, curated dataset.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Goal:</strong> Adapt the model to a specific task (e.g., writing code, speaking medical jargon) or style (e.g., &quot;always answer in JSON&quot;).</li>
<li><strong>Cost:</strong> Hundreds to thousands of dollars.</li>
<li><strong>Outcome:</strong> A model that is better at <em>one thing</em> but potentially worse at general tasks.</li>
</ul>
<p>Fine-tuning changes the model&#39;s weights. It is permanent. It is useful when you need the model to learn a new &quot;grammar&quot; or &quot;behavior&quot; that is too complex to explain in a prompt.</p>
<h2 id="act-ii-the-modern-paradigm">Act II: The modern paradigm</h2>
<h3 id="inference-the-runtime">Inference: The Runtime</h3>
<p><strong>Inference</strong> is what happens when you send a prompt to ChatGPT or an API. The model is &quot;frozen.&quot; It does not change. It simply processes your input and predicts the output.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Goal:</strong> Solve a specific problem right now.</li>
<li><strong>Cost:</strong> Fractions of a cent per request.</li>
<li><strong>Outcome:</strong> Immediate value.</li>
</ul>
<p>Crucially, <strong>inference is stateless</strong>. If you tell the model &quot;My name is Sarah&quot; in one chat, and then open a new chat, it does not know your name. It did not &quot;learn&quot; anything; it just held your name in its temporary working memory (context window) for that session.</p>
<h2 id="act-iii-principles-in-practice">Act III: Principles in practice</h2>
<h3 id="why-you-probably-dont-need-to-train">Why You Probably Don&#39;t Need to Train</h3>
<p>Business leaders often say, &quot;We need to train a model on our documents.&quot;</p>
<p>What they usually mean is: &quot;We need the model to <em>know</em> about our documents.&quot;</p>
<p>You do not achieve this by training. You achieve this by <strong>Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)</strong>.</p>

<h3 id="what-this-changes-in-practice">What this changes in practice</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Stop asking for &quot;training&quot;:</strong> Ask for &quot;context&quot; or &quot;knowledge retrieval.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Use RAG for facts:</strong> If you want the model to know your company policy, put the policy in the prompt (or use RAG). Do not fine-tune on it. Fine-tuning is for <em>behavior</em>, not <em>knowledge</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Treat prompts as transient:</strong> Remember that nothing you type into the model sticks. If you want persistence, you must build it into your application layer (database), not the model layer.</li>
</ol>
