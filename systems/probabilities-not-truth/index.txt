<p><strong>Key takeaways</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Large Language Models (LLMs) are <strong>probabilistic</strong>, not deterministic. They do not know what is true; they know what is <span class="highlight">likely</span>.</li>
<li><strong>Hallucination</strong> is not a malfunction; it is the model doing exactly what it was trained to do (predict the next token) without a grounding in reality.</li>
<li>Confidence is a style, not a metric of accuracy. A model can be 100% confident and 100% wrong.</li>
<li>To build reliable systems, you must treat model outputs as <strong>proposals</strong>, not facts.</li>
</ul>

<p>If you ask a database for the capital of France, it looks up the row labeled &quot;France&quot; and returns &quot;Paris.&quot; If the row doesn&#39;t exist, it returns an error.</p>
<p>If you ask an AI model for the capital of Mars, it doesn&#39;t look up a fact. It calculates which words are statistically most likely to follow the phrase &quot;The capital of Mars is...&quot; based on the science fiction and speculative texts it has read. It might confidently answer &quot;Elon City&quot; or &quot;Olympus Mons.&quot;</p>
<p>This is the fundamental nature of the technology: it is a <strong>probability engine</strong>, not a truth engine.</p>
<h3 id="why-do-models-sound-true-when-they-are-only-probable">Why do models sound true when they are only probable?</h3>
<p>Models sound true because they are trained to continue authoritative language patterns, not to verify reality. This page is for readers who need a safer mental model for using AI in products and decisions, and the practical shift is to treat outputs as proposals that must be grounded, checked, or constrained.</p>

<h2 id="act-i-the-fundamentals">Act I: The fundamentals</h2>
<h3 id="the-confidence-trick">The Confidence Trick</h3>
<p>Why do models sound so confident even when they are wrong?</p>
<p>Because they are trained on human writing, and humans usually write confidently. The model is mimicking the <strong>style</strong> of an authoritative answer.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Training Data:</strong> Wikipedia, textbooks, news articles (all authoritative styles).</li>
<li><strong>Model Behavior:</strong> Predicts the next token that fits that authoritative style.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you ask a question, the most probable continuation is a confident answer, not a hesitant one. The model isn&#39;t &quot;feeling&quot; confident; it is simply completing the pattern of a confident expert.</p>
<h3 id="hallucination-is-a-feature-not-a-bug">Hallucination Is a Feature, Not a Bug</h3>
<p>We call it &quot;hallucination&quot; when the model makes things up. But to the model, there is no difference between &quot;recalling a fact&quot; and &quot;inventing a fact.&quot; Both are just predicting the next token.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Recall:</strong> &quot;The capital of France is [Paris].&quot; (High probability because &quot;Paris&quot; almost always follows).</li>
<li><strong>Hallucination:</strong> &quot;The first person on Mars was [John Boone].&quot; (High probability because in the sci-fi context it read, this was true).</li>
</ul>
<p>The model is designed to be <strong>creative</strong> and <strong>fluent</strong>. The same mechanism that allows it to write a fictional story (a feature) allows it to invent a legal precedent (a bug). You cannot easily turn off one without killing the other.</p>
<aside class="callout">
  <p><strong>Insight.</strong> Hallucination is just "creativity" applied to the wrong context.</p>
</aside>
<h2 id="act-ii-the-modern-paradigm">Act II: The modern paradigm</h2>
<h3 id="accuracy-vs-trustworthiness">Accuracy vs Trustworthiness</h3>
<p>In traditional software, we expect 100% accuracy. If a calculator says 2+2=5, it is broken.</p>
<p>In AI, we cannot expect 100% accuracy. Instead, we must design for <strong>trustworthiness</strong>.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Accuracy:</strong> Getting the right answer.</li>
<li><strong>Trustworthiness:</strong> Knowing when you might be wrong.</li>
</ul>
<p>Current models are high-accuracy but low-trustworthiness. They are often right, but they don&#39;t know when they are wrong. This makes them dangerous if used without supervision.</p>
<h2 id="act-iii-principles-in-practice">Act III: Principles in practice</h2>
<p>The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty, but to design around it. If the system is probabilistic, then your workflows must be explicit about verification, constraints, and fallback paths.</p>
<p>For related systems context, see <a href="/systems/systems-001-foundations/">Systems 001: Foundations</a> and <a href="/systems/from-prompt-to-production/">From Prompt to Production</a>. For grounding and retrieval patterns, see <a href="/systems/retrieval-augmented-generation-in-plain-terms/">Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Plain Terms</a>. To see how validation and evaluation datasets are used to measure uncertainty and grounding in practice, refer to the <a href="/shelf/shared-resources/retrieval-and-grounding-evaluation-kit/">retrieval and grounding evaluation kit</a>.</p>
<h3 id="what-this-changes-in-practice">What this changes in practice</h3>
<p>Since we cannot rely on the model to be truthful, we must change how we build systems around it:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Grounding (RAG):</strong> Don&#39;t ask the model to &quot;know&quot; things. Give it a document and ask it to &quot;extract&quot; things. This forces it to use the provided context rather than its internal probabilities.</li>
<li><strong>Verification:</strong> Never use an LLM for a high-stakes decision without a verification step (either a human review or a code-based check).</li>
<li><strong>Citations:</strong> Ask the model to cite its sources. If it can&#39;t point to where it found the information in your provided text, it is likely hallucinating.</li>
</ol>
<p>Treat the model like a brilliant but prone-to-exaggeration intern. You give them the task, but you check their work.</p>
