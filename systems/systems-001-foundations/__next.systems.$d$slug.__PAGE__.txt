<p>A system is a set of interconnected components (physical or abstract) that work together to achieve a purpose. It has inputs, processing, and outputs, and it is shaped by boundaries, constraints, and feedback. You can see systems in circulatory networks, in software infrastructure, and in the governing rules that coordinate human activity. Systems theory studies these patterns across domains because the logic repeats even when the material changes (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_systems_theory">von Bertalanffy, 1968</a>).</p>
<h3 id="what-is-a-system-in-practical-terms">What is a system, in practical terms?</h3>
<p>A system is a repeatable arrangement of parts, boundaries, signals, and incentives that produces behavior over time. This guide is for readers who want a durable mental model before they dive into AI architecture, operations, or governance, and it is meant to make the later applied pages easier to interpret.</p>
<aside class="callout">
  <p><strong>Definition.</strong> A system is any collection of organized, interacting parts that forms a functional whole. Its behavior is shaped by components, boundaries, feedback, and purpose.</p>
</aside>

<h2 id="act-i-the-fundamentals">Act I: The fundamentals</h2>
<h3 id="core-concepts">Core concepts</h3>
<p>Every system can be described with five primitives. If you can name these, you can map the system quickly and consistently.</p>
<table class="comparison-table">
  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>Primitive</th>
      <th>What it is</th>
      <th>Why it matters</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>Components</td>
      <td>The parts that do the work.</td>
      <td>Clarifies ownership and dependencies.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Connections</td>
      <td>How parts exchange signals, material, or decisions.</td>
      <td>Shows coupling, delay, and fragility.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Boundary</td>
      <td>What the system takes in and pushes out.</td>
      <td>Defines scope, responsibility, and risk.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Feedback</td>
      <td>Signals that change behavior over time.</td>
      <td>Explains adaptation or drift.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Purpose</td>
      <td>The outcome the system optimizes for.</td>
      <td>Makes tradeoffs and incentives legible.</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
<p>Key characteristics show up everywhere:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Emerges</strong>: the whole behaves differently than the parts.</li>
<li><strong>Adapts</strong>: feedback changes the system over time.</li>
<li><strong>Trades off</strong>: every optimization hides a cost.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="boundaries-interfaces-constraints">Boundaries, interfaces, constraints</h3>
<p>Boundaries are the system’s edges. Interfaces are how those edges interact. Constraints are the limits that keep the system safe or possible.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Boundary</strong> answers: what is inside and what is outside?</li>
<li><strong>Interface</strong> answers: how do inputs and outputs flow?</li>
<li><strong>Constraint</strong> answers: what cannot change without breaking the system?</li>
</ul>
<p>Good interfaces expose the right degrees of freedom and hide the rest. When interfaces leak, systems become brittle. When constraints are ignored, systems drift.</p>
<h3 id="failure-modes-and-resilience">Failure modes and resilience</h3>
<p>Systems fail in repeatable patterns. This table is the minimal checklist for diagnosing drift early and designing for recovery.</p>
<table class="comparison-table">
  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>Failure mode</th>
      <th>Early signal</th>
      <th>Design response</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>Drift</td>
      <td>Metrics improve while outcomes worsen</td>
      <td>Recalibrate incentives and refresh assumptions</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Goodhart's Law</td>
      <td>Teams optimize the proxy, not the goal</td>
      <td>Add qualitative checks and multi-metric balance</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Variety mismatch</td>
      <td>Edge cases dominate incident load</td>
      <td>Increase system flexibility or human oversight</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
<aside class="callout callout-insight">
  <p><strong>Resilience.</strong> A resilient system can sense, respond, and learn. That means observability, incident response, and clear human escalation paths are part of the system—not optional tooling.</p>
</aside>
<h4 id="open-closed-and-isolated">Open, closed, and isolated</h4>
<p>Systems are also described by how they interact with their environment:</p>
<table class="comparison-table">
  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>System type</th>
      <th>Interaction</th>
      <th>Why it matters</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>Open</td>
      <td>Exchanges energy or information with the outside world.</td>
      <td>Most real systems are open; feedback is essential.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Closed</td>
      <td>Bounded and self-contained in theory.</td>
      <td>Useful for modeling, rare in practice.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Isolated</td>
      <td>Fully cut off from the environment.</td>
      <td>Mostly conceptual; systems drift without feedback.</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
<h3 id="systems-as-the-backbone-of-history">Systems as the backbone of history</h3>
<p>Human history is a sequence of system upgrades:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Physical coordination</strong>: irrigation, roads, calendars.</li>
<li><strong>Symbolic systems</strong>: alphabets, accounting, maps.</li>
<li><strong>Communication infrastructure</strong>: printing, telegraphy, networks.</li>
<li><strong>Computational systems</strong>: programmable logic, software, automation.</li>
<li><strong>Socio-technical systems</strong>: humans and machines operating as one loop.</li>
</ul>
<p>Shannon formalized information as a measurable signal, and Wiener formalized feedback and control, making adaptation explicit rather than accidental (<a href="https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf">Shannon, 1948</a>; <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics:_Or_Control_and_Communication_in_the_Animal_and_the_Machine">Wiener, 1948</a>). These are milestones in how systems learn.</p>

<h2 id="act-ii-the-modern-paradigm">Act II: The modern paradigm</h2>
<h3 id="the-socio-technical-merge">The socio-technical merge</h3>
<p>The old divide between &quot;people&quot; and &quot;software&quot; has evolved into a <span class="highlight">unified socio-technical system</span>: human intent, organizational culture, and machine execution are interdependent (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socio-technical_systems">Trist &amp; Bamforth, 1951</a>).</p>
<p>This matters because technical work is no longer just code. It is the behavior of a composite system where algorithms, teams, and governance are part of one operating loop.</p>
<figure class="diagram">
  <iframe src="/scenes/sociotechnical-layers.html" title="Socio-technical layers" loading="lazy" scrolling="no" style="pointer-events: none;"></iframe>
  <figcaption>The system is layered, but the behavior is unified.</figcaption>
</figure>
<h4 id="level-1-the-machine-layer-deterministic-and-agentic">Level 1: the machine layer (deterministic and agentic)</h4>
<p>This layer covers how the system perceives and acts. Modern systems add decision-centric behavior: simulate outcomes, score tradeoffs, and recommend actions (<a href="https://www.tf-pm.org/resources">Process Mining community resources</a>).</p>
<p>Document how the system captures context:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Semantic context</strong>: what the data means, not just how it is formatted (<a href="https://www.w3.org/RDF/">W3C RDF</a>, <a href="https://www.w3.org/OWL/">W3C OWL</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Temporal context</strong>: when the data is valid, captured, and acted upon (<a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/owl-time/">W3C Time Ontology</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Active metadata</strong>: metadata that changes as systems learn and humans intervene (<a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-overview/">W3C PROV</a>).</li>
</ul>
<p>Agentic behavior raises the bar: specify what the system can do, what it cannot do, and how it signals uncertainty.</p>
<h4 id="level-2-the-human-layer-strategic-and-ethical">Level 2: the human layer (strategic and ethical)</h4>
<p>This layer defines who is accountable and when human judgment is required.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Human-in-the-loop (HITL)</strong>: humans are part of the decision process, approving or rejecting system outputs.</li>
<li><strong>Human-on-the-loop (HOTL)</strong>: humans supervise the system and step in during anomalies or edge cases.</li>
<li><strong>Human-out-of-the-loop (HOOTL)</strong>: systems act autonomously after initial parameters are defined.</li>
</ul>
<p>These are operational constraints. High-risk systems now require explicit human oversight (<a href="https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_24_3031">EU AI Act, 2024</a>; <a href="https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework">NIST AI RMF 1.0</a>).</p>
<p>In documentation, make the decision boundary explicit: where the system stops and a person decides.</p>
<h4 id="level-3-interconnectivity-socio-technical">Level 3: interconnectivity (socio-technical)</h4>
<p>Here, social and technical layers fuse into one behavior. Teams shape the system; the system shapes teams.</p>
<p>Two patterns dominate:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Governance as code</strong>: policy becomes enforceable rules (<a href="https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/">OPA Documentation</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Digital twins and simulation</strong>: test decisions before committing resources (<a href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120008178">Shafto et al., 2012</a>).</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="act-iii-principles-in-practice">Act III: Principles in practice</h2>
<h3 id="systems-thinking-in-business-strategy">Systems thinking in business strategy</h3>
<p>Organizations are systems. Applied well, systems thinking helps leaders:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Find root causes</strong> instead of treating symptoms.</li>
<li><strong>Map feedback loops</strong> that amplify or dampen outcomes.</li>
<li><strong>Identify bottlenecks</strong> that limit throughput or quality.</li>
<li><strong>Design for emergence</strong>, creating conditions for culture and trust to grow.</li>
</ul>
<p>The difference is perspective: linear optimizes locally; systems optimize the whole.</p>
<h3 id="documentation-as-system-design">Documentation as system design</h3>
<p>Documentation is part of the system: it shapes how the system is built, understood, and governed.</p>
<p>Here is what changes in modern technical writing:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Describe intent, not just implementation.</strong> The system must be able to explain itself.</li>
<li><strong>Capture context.</strong> Document semantic, temporal, and provenance context so system outputs can be trusted across time.</li>
<li><strong>Model human oversight.</strong> State when humans must intervene and how decisions are escalated.</li>
<li><strong>Encode policy.</strong> Show how governance is enforced in code and audited in practice.</li>
<li><strong>Simulate the future.</strong> Include model limitations, simulation assumptions, and expected failure modes.</li>
</ol>

<aside class="callout callout-insight">
  <p><strong>Insight.</strong> A system that cannot explain itself cannot be governed. Documentation is not a byproduct; it is the interface between a system and its human layer.</p>
</aside>
<h3 id="systems-as-compression">Systems as compression</h3>
<p>Every system compresses complexity into rules. Compression makes communication scalable, but it trades nuance for speed. Good systems manage that tradeoff; bad systems distort reality.</p>
<p>Compression is also a value choice: schemas, metrics, and routing rules decide what counts.</p>
<h3 id="systems-as-feedback">Systems as feedback</h3>
<p>Feedback is the evolutionary engine. Without it, a system is a black box; with it, a system becomes governable. In socio-technical systems, feedback is both technical and social: people interpret signals and change behavior.</p>

<h3 id="the-system-is-the-message">The system is the message</h3>
<p>When execution and decision-making converge, systems shape reality. The system that filters information filters the future. The system that encodes policy decides what is possible.</p>
<p>So the goal is not just working systems, but systems that are understandable, governable, and adaptable.</p>
<h3 id="putting-systems-thinking-into-practice">Putting systems thinking into practice</h3>
<p>Use these prompts to audit any system you touch:</p>
<table class="comparison-table">
  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>Question</th>
      <th>What to look for</th>
      <th>Design response</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>Where are the boundaries?</td>
      <td>Inputs/outputs without owners, leaky interfaces, silent dependencies.</td>
      <td>Define interfaces, assign ownership, document what stays outside.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>What are the feedback loops?</td>
      <td>Delayed signals, missing instrumentation, manual backchannels.</td>
      <td>Shorten loops, add observability, make signals visible.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Where does human intent enter?</td>
      <td>Ambiguous handoffs or decisions made by default.</td>
      <td>Mark decision boundaries, add approval gates where needed.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Which metric is optimized?</td>
      <td>Proxy metrics driving behavior instead of outcomes.</td>
      <td>Balance with counter-metrics and qualitative checks.</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Where is variety mismatch?</td>
      <td>Edge cases dominate incidents or escalations.</td>
      <td>Add flexibility, escalation paths, or human oversight.</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
