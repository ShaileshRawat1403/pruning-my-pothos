<p><strong>Key takeaways</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Workflows prioritize predictability; agents prioritize adaptive step selection.</li>
<li>Most production systems are hybrids: agent-like reasoning inside workflow boundaries.</li>
<li>The real design question is not &quot;agent or workflow?&quot; but &quot;where is autonomy allowed, and who governs it?&quot;</li>
<li>Reliability improves when model freedom is scoped by policy, verification, and clear escalation paths.</li>
</ul>
</blockquote>
<p>This article explains a core component of modern AI systems: the difference between AI agents and AI workflows. It focuses on orchestration, runtime freedom, policy boundaries, and why this distinction matters for reliability.</p>
<p>It is most useful for teams deciding how much autonomy to allow in production systems, and for builders who need a calmer language for discussing control instead of hype.</p>
<p>The phrase &quot;AI agent&quot; has become so broad that it often stops being useful. Sometimes it means tool use. Sometimes it means multi-step planning. Sometimes it means a chatbot with memory. That vagueness creates expensive architecture mistakes because teams start debating labels instead of control surfaces.</p>
<h3 id="what-is-the-practical-difference-between-agents-and-workflows">What is the practical difference between agents and workflows?</h3>
<p>An AI workflow is a structured path with predefined states, transitions, and checks. An AI agent is a system that can propose or choose its next step inside a goal. In practice, good production systems use both: agent-like reasoning to decide locally, and workflow constraints to keep the system accountable.</p>
<p>In practice, <span class="highlight">autonomy without boundaries is usually just hidden uncertainty</span>.</p>
<aside class="callout callout-insight">
  <p><strong>Design note.</strong> Ask "what kind of decision freedom exists at runtime?" before asking whether something deserves the word agent. That question leads to better systems conversations.</p>
</aside>

<h2 id="act-i-the-distinction">Act I: The distinction</h2>
<h3 id="why-the-terms-get-mixed-up">Why the terms get mixed up</h3>
<p>There are good reasons the terms blur:</p>
<ul>
<li>workflows can now include model reasoning</li>
<li>agents often call tools using step sequences that look like workflows</li>
<li>product marketing tends to reward the word &quot;agent&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>But architecture still needs the distinction because the two patterns create different operational responsibilities. If the runtime freedom is small, you can test it like a workflow. If the runtime freedom is large, you need stronger policy and observability.</p>
<h3 id="a-clearer-definition">A clearer definition</h3>
<p>The difference is easiest to see through runtime choice.</p>
<ul>
<li>A <strong>workflow</strong> has a mostly known path. The model may fill in fields, classify inputs, or summarize outputs, but the surrounding system still decides the path.</li>
<li>An <strong>agent</strong> has meaningful local freedom. It can choose among tools, sequence steps, or revise its plan based on intermediate results.</li>
</ul>
<p>That freedom can be useful. It can also create hidden complexity. Once a system decides its own next move, you have to govern that decision.</p>
<h2 id="act-ii-runtime-behavior">Act II: Runtime behavior</h2>
<h3 id="how-workflows-behave">How workflows behave</h3>
<p>Workflows are good at repeatable tasks with known states:</p>
<ul>
<li>intake and triage</li>
<li>document extraction</li>
<li>policy enforcement</li>
<li>constrained support tasks</li>
</ul>
<p>The advantage is control. Teams can test expected branches, attach explicit approvals, and keep changes diffable over time.</p>

<h3 id="how-agents-behave">How agents behave</h3>
<p>Agent-like systems become useful when the path is not fully known in advance. They can:</p>
<ul>
<li>choose among tools</li>
<li>loop through intermediate evidence</li>
<li>adjust the next step based on results</li>
<li>ask for clarification when the goal is underspecified</li>
</ul>
<p>That flexibility helps with exploratory or multi-step work. But it also raises the burden of evaluation. Teams now need to inspect not only final output, but the quality of intermediate decisions.</p>
<h3 id="why-most-production-systems-are-hybrids">Why most production systems are hybrids</h3>
<p>This is the part many architecture debates miss. Useful systems are rarely pure workflows or pure agents. They are usually hybrids:</p>
<ul>
<li>a workflow sets the allowed path and checkpoints</li>
<li>an agent-like component proposes the best next local step</li>
<li>the runtime applies policy and verification before execution</li>
</ul>
<p>That hybrid pattern is the most practical because it lets teams use language-model flexibility without surrendering system discipline. It is the same design logic behind <a href="/systems/from-agent-intent-to-governed-execution/">From Agent Intent to Governed Execution</a> and <a href="/systems/agent-instructions-and-handoff-as-an-operating-system/">Agent Instructions and Handoff as an Operating System</a>.</p>

<h2 id="act-iii-choosing-the-right-pattern">Act III: Choosing the right pattern</h2>
<h3 id="when-to-prefer-a-workflow">When to prefer a workflow</h3>
<p>Prefer workflow-first design when:</p>
<ul>
<li>the task has known states</li>
<li>policy violations are expensive</li>
<li>output contracts must stay stable</li>
<li>you need easy rollback and audit</li>
</ul>
<p>Many enterprise use cases fall into this category. Teams still use models, but the path should remain tightly governed.</p>
<h3 id="when-agent-like-control-is-worth-it">When agent-like control is worth it</h3>
<p>Agent-like control earns its keep when the system must discover or negotiate the next step:</p>
<ul>
<li>open-ended research</li>
<li>repo understanding</li>
<li>multi-tool diagnostic loops</li>
<li>complex drafting or synthesis with verification</li>
</ul>
<p>Even then, autonomy should remain local. The runtime should still own permissions, verification, and step recording. Soothsayer is a good example of this principle. It is more useful as a controlled orchestration system than as an unconstrained &quot;agent.&quot;</p>
<h3 id="what-this-changes-in-practice">What this changes in practice</h3>
<p>Stop asking whether your product is &quot;an agent.&quot; Ask where runtime freedom is genuinely valuable, and where predictability is more important. Most durable AI products are workflows with carefully scoped agent behavior inside them.</p>
